Step 1 of 4

The pixels nobody wants

SPHEREx photographs the whole sky every six months in 102 infrared colours. In 73% of its exposures a satellite crosses the field. The pipeline marks the streak with a flag and scientists mask it out. Those pixels are, officially, waste.

Step 2 of 4

Two cameras, one instant

Behind the telescope a dichroic mirror sends short wavelengths to one camera and long wavelengths to another. Every streak is therefore recorded twice, at the same moment. In reflected sunlight it is faint. In heat it blazes.

Step 3 of 4

It glows with its own heat

Light leaks out either side of the masked core. We measure it, calibrate it against the stars, and fit the long-wavelength slope. The object is not just reflecting the Sun. It is warm.

Step 4 of 4

And it has a name

The image header records where SPHEREx was at the start, middle and end of the exposure. We propagated all 31,500 catalogued objects across those 115 seconds. Exactly one track lies on the streak and moves the same way.

H-1 rocket body (MABES) — a Japanese upper stage launched in 1986. NORAD 16910. 987 km from the telescope, 1,508 km above the Earth. It crossed at 04:34:14 UTC, in 4.4 seconds. SPHEREx's own “first half of the exposure” flag agrees.

How it works, and what it cannot do

Flipbook

Every SPHEREx exposure covering the position is found through NASA/IPAC IRSA, cut out, reprojected to one north-up grid and grouped into sky passes. All images of one band share one brightness scale, so a change you see is real. What changed subtracts one pass from the next: a moving star becomes a blue–amber pair, a variable star brightens or fades.

Temperature

The flagged core of a streak is unusable; its edges are not. We measure the edge light in both cameras of a detector pair, scale it with the stars in the same frames, and fit a blackbody to the 4.4–5.0 µm slope with a bootstrap range. Ratios depend on the method by ±30%; the temperature is far more stable, so we show ratios as indicative and temperatures with ranges.

Name

SPHEREx's position at the start, middle and end of each exposure is in the file header. We propagate the public satellite catalogue (Space-Track elements, SGP4) across the exposure and keep an object only if its apparent track lies on the streak and runs the same way. Both showcase streaks matched exactly one object. Orbit data for the exposure date is required and cannot be redistributed, so live naming depends on the server's orbit file.

Limits

One temperature is an average over the visible surface; size and emissivity cannot be separated. Naming only works for catalogued objects. The temperature needs the long-wavelength cameras (D5/D6) to see the streak. Each streak crosses one detector pair, so a single satellite yields two of the six bands, not a full spectrum.
